# Reading sellers and spotting bad listings

## Is a seller with lots of phones a problem?

Volume alone isn't the signal — composition is. Three patterns to tell apart:

**The household clear-out.** Thirty-plus ads across unrelated categories: a parking space, a stroller, a rice cooker, a jacket, one phone. This is someone emptying a flat. Normal, low risk. Judge the listing on its own merits.

**The hobbyist flipper.** Ten to thirty ads, mostly electronics, a handful of phones over several months, varied models and storage tiers, descriptions written by hand and specific to each device. Buying and reselling as a side activity. Fine to deal with, but the "facture" they hold is the *original owner's* invoice — it proves the phone's provenance, not a sale to them, so warranty transfer is real but the seller themself owes nothing after the handshake.

**The undeclared reseller.** The one to be wary of: many phones live at once, often the same two or three models in different colours, near-identical description templates, stock-style photos or the same backdrop repeated, prices clustered just under retail, immediate availability, a push toward WhatsApp or off-platform payment. Someone trading as a business while registered as a *particulier*. In France that matters practically, not just legally — buying from a declared professional gives you a 2-year garantie légale de conformité, a real invoice in your name, and a company to go back to. Buying from someone impersonating a private seller gives you none of that, and a bulk supply of identical recent flagships raises the obvious question of where they came from.

So: many *different* things → fine. Many *of the same phone* → treat the ad as a shop listing without any of a shop's protections, and either walk away or insist on an invoice photo, the IMEI, and a blacklist check before money moves.

A high review count with a good score is mild reassurance. A brand-new profile with one expensive phone is not damning on its own, but it removes the only cheap reassurance available — weight the other signals harder.

## Listing red flags

Any one of these is worth a mention to the user; two or more means say "avoid".

**Spec sheet doesn't match the model.** The tell that catches the most bad listings. A "Galaxy S25 512 Go" whose description boasts a 6.8" 4K screen, 200 MP camera and 10x zoom is describing an Ultra — the text was copy-pasted from another ad, so nothing in it can be trusted, including the storage and the invoice claim. Cross-check headline specs against the model actually being sold.

**Urgency and sales theatre.** Emoji-laden titles, "prix imbattable", "vs 1082 € en boutique", "offre limitée, premier venu premier servi", "réponse sous 1h". Private sellers with one phone to shift rarely write ad copy.

**Price too far below market.** A genuine 20-30% discount happens. A 60% discount on a current flagship does not. The gap is the bait.

**Pressure to leave the platform.** WhatsApp, Telegram, a personal email, "je préfère traiter en direct". Leboncoin's secured payment and message history are the only recourse a buyer has; leaving them is the point of the request.

**Payment-method games.** Asking for a deposit to "reserve" it, a link to a lookalike payment page, a courier the seller insists on, an offer to pay you back the difference. The fake-secured-payment scam is common enough that any payment link arriving by message should be assumed hostile.

**No box, no charger, no invoice, but priced like new.** Not fraud, just a bad deal — say so.

**Stale ad.** Published months ago and never updated. Frequently already sold. Worth a message before travelling, not worth topping the list.

**Location inconsistency.** The phone is in Puteaux, the seller's other twenty ads are all in Lyon. Sometimes innocent, sometimes an ad recycled from someone else's listing.

## What an invoice actually buys

Worth stating plainly when the user asks why it matters:

- The remaining manufacturer warranty follows the device, so a dated invoice naming the retailer is what makes a warranty claim possible at all.
- It's evidence of lawful provenance. It doesn't prove it — invoices are forged, and an invoice in a third party's name proves nothing about the person holding the phone — but its absence on a recent flagship is a genuine question mark.
- It fixes the model, storage and purchase date, which is what makes a price comparison meaningful.

What it does *not* buy: any obligation from the seller after the sale. Between private individuals there is no garantie légale de conformité and no right of withdrawal. The invoice is a claim against the manufacturer, not against the person you met in a car park.

Practical checks, in order of value: photo of the invoice showing date and IMEI/serial before travelling; `*#06#` on the phone to confirm the IMEI matches; a stolen-device blacklist check on that IMEI; the phone powered on and signed out of the seller's account in front of you.
